# Design Taste — frontend art direction

The goal is not to decorate a template. Make deliberate choices that fit the actual client, audience, brand, and content.

## 1. Read the brief before choosing a style
Identify:
- What the business or product actually does and who the page serves.
- The primary action the visitor should take.
- Existing brand assets: logo, colors, typography, photos, and real project examples.
- The stack and conventions already used by the project.
- The user's visual references and explicit dislikes.

State a short design direction before implementation. Ask one question only if a missing decision would materially change the result; otherwise make a reasonable, stated choice.

## 2. Find a concept specific to the subject
Write down 5–10 details that belong to this business and not every competitor: name meaning, place, materials, process, product shape, local environment, customer ritual, or distinctive constraint. Build the visual idea from one of those details rather than from a generic industry template.

Before building, complete this sentence:
> On this page, [a real detail from this subject] becomes [a visual device that supports the concept].

If the sentence is generic enough to fit any company, keep exploring.

## 3. Compose intentionally
- Choose a clear hierarchy and a visual rhythm; do not default to a centered headline plus three equal cards.
- Vary alignment, scale, whitespace, section density, image crops, and grid proportions when the brief supports it.
- Use typography with a reason: display face for character, readable body face, restrained weights, and deliberate line lengths.
- Choose a coherent palette from the subject and brand. Avoid automatic purple/blue gradients and random accent colors.
- Prefer authentic project photography, product images, and credible contextual imagery. Never present stock or generated imagery as a client's real work.
- Use texture, shape, or motion only when it strengthens the concept.
- Every section must have a purpose. Do not add logo strips, testimonials, statistics, pricing, or feature cards without real content or a clear user need.
- Avoid both extremes: generic template density and empty minimalism. The page needs enough real content and visual substance to feel finished.

## 4. Respect the existing project
- For redesigns, inspect the current page and preserve working behavior, content, links, brand assets, and stack unless the brief says otherwise.
- Do not replace an existing design system or add a framework without a concrete reason.
- Avoid rewriting unrelated files. Reuse components and styles where appropriate.
- Keep navigation, forms, CTAs, and responsive behavior functional.
- Never invent customer reviews, client results, prices, addresses, credentials, or contact information.

## 5. Mobile-first quality
- Design for the smallest supported viewport first, then enhance for larger screens.
- Check navigation, heading wraps, tap targets, image crops, overflow, spacing, and CTA visibility on mobile.
- Use responsive type and layout rather than shrinking desktop composition.
- Support keyboard focus, readable contrast, semantic structure, and `prefers-reduced-motion`.
- Motion should communicate hierarchy or state; keep it restrained and provide a reduced-motion alternative.

## 6. Inspect the result
When tools are available, inspect the actual rendered page at mobile and desktop widths. Look for:
- Generic patterns that ignore the brief.
- Weak or inconsistent hierarchy and typography.
- Empty sections, unbalanced composition, awkward line breaks, and repetitive cards.
- Low-quality or irrelevant imagery.
- Broken navigation, CTAs, or responsive layouts.
- Contrast, focus, and reduced-motion issues.

Fix the highest-impact issues first, then inspect again. Do not claim visual validation if no browser or screenshot check was performed.

## Completion
Report the key design decisions and any checks actually performed. Prefer specific explanations over claims such as “modern,” “beautiful,” or “premium.”
